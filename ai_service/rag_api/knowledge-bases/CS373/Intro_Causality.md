## Introduction to Causal Modeling 
#### Understanding the Data Generation Process


In this course, we have often used libraries to sample random variables

- **But how do these libraries work?**

### Data Generation Process (i)
**[Inversion transform sampling method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_transform_sampling)** 

Wikipedia example for exponential distribution $P(X \leq x) = F_{X}(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}$ with inverse $x=F_X^{-1}(r)=-{\frac {1}{\lambda }}\ln(1-r)$:
<center><img src="InverseTransform.png" width=350px/></center>

- This is the most fundamental technique for generating sample values of random variables

- It uses the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the random variable

- The method depends on the fact that, for any random variable X, the CDF, $F_X(x) = P(X \leq x)$, is a non-decreasing function of $x$ that outputs a number in the interval \[0,1\]

- Let $F_X^{-1}$ be the inverse of $F_X$, i.e., $x = F_X^{-1}(F_X(x))$.

- Let $r \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ be a random uniform value in the interval \[0,1\]
    - This is obtained by a [pseudorandom number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator)

- Then, $$ x_\text{sample} = F_X^{-1}(r)$$ is a random sample with distribution $P(X = x)$.

### Data Generation Process (ii)

Any probability distribution $$𝑃(𝑌)$$ can be described as the data generated by the inverse transform sampling
$$𝑌=F_Y^{-1}(U),$$   
where
$$F_Y^{-1} \text{ is a deterministic function}$$
and $$U∼\text{Uniform}(0,1)$$ is some independent uniform random noise.

*Notation*: $a \sim b$ means $a$ is "sampled from distribution" $b$

### Data Generation Process (iii)
#### Conditional Distributions

Any **conditional** probability distribution $$P(Y|X=x)$$ can be described as $$Y = F_{Y|X}^{-1}(x,U)$$ for $$U∼\text{Uniform}(0,1).$$

### Describing Joint Probability Distributions

Let $Y,T,S \in \{0,1\}$ be three binary random variables.

Consider the following interpretation.
- $Y$ = treatment positive outcome {0,1}
- $T$ = treatment {A,B}
- $S$ = kidney stone size.

Suppose we use hospital information as training data for our statistical model: $\mathcal{D}=\{(y_i,t_i,s_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ for each patient $i$.

The [*chain rule* of probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_rule_(probability)) states 
$$ P(A,B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A).$$

Hence, the joint probability distribution of $(Y,T,S)$ is 
$$
P(Y,T,S)
$$
and can be decomposed as
$$
P(Y|T,S)P(T|S)P(S)
$$
or
$$
P(S|Y,T)P(Y|T)P(T)
$$
or
$$
P(S|T,Y)P(T|Y)P(Y)
$$
or
...

### On the Data Generation Process

**A joint probability distribution is simply a way to assign probabilities to joint events**

We should **never** use conditional distributions to interpret how the data was generated. 

**Q:** Why? 

**A:** Because for any of the following data generation processes **describes the training data equally well:**

1. A data generation process based on the decomposition $P(Y|T,S)P(T|S)P(S)$:

$$\begin{align} S&=F^{-1}_S(U_S),\\  
T&=F^{-1}_T(S,U_T),\\
Y&=F^{-1}_Y(T,S,U_Y),\end{align}$$   

where $U_S, U_T, U_Y \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ are uniform variables sampled indepedently.

2. A data generation process based on the decomposition $P(Y|T,S)P(S|T)P(T)$:

$$\begin{align} T&=F^{-1}_T(U_T),\\  
S&=F^{-1}_S(T,U_S),\\
Y&=F^{-1}_Y(T,S,U_Y),\end{align}$$   

where $U_S, U_T, U_Y \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ sampled indepedently.

3. A data generation process based on the decomposition $P(Y|T,S)P(S)P(T)$ that assumes $P(S|T)=P(S)$:

$$\begin{align} T&=F^{-1}_T(U_T),\\  
S&=F^{-1}_S(U_S),\\
Y&=F^{-1}_Y(T,S,U_Y),\end{align}$$   

where $U_S, U_T, U_Y \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ sampled indepedently.

Hence, we cannot predict what happens if we force $T = A$ in the data generation process (force treatment to be "A"):
$$
P(Y,S|do(T=A))
$$

- `do()` notation: The DO notation asks what would happen if we forced a variable to have a certain value. This is the notation developed by Judea Pearl.

**Alternative notation:** An alternative notation for $P(Y,S|do(T=A))$ is $$
P(Y(T=A),S)
$$ which is the notation used by Guido Imbens.

**Quiz 1:** Which data generation process is more likely to describe our hospital data?


**A.** In the data generation process

$$\begin{align} S&=F^{-1}_S(U_S),\\  
T&=F^{-1}_T(S,U_T),\\
Y&=F^{-1}_Y(T,S,U_Y),\end{align}$$   

the "do" operation is forcing $T=A$, hence the data is generated as
$$\begin{align} S&=F^{-1}_S(U_S),\\

Y&=F^{-1}_Y(T,S,U_Y).\end{align}$$

**C.** In yet another data generation process, the "do(T=A)" operation gets the following data
$$\begin{align} T&=A,\\  
S&=F^{-1}_S(U_S),\\

### The Dangers of Data-driven Machine Learning

<center><img src="Kidney0.png" width=600/></center>

In our dataset, treatment B appears more effective than treatment A:
$$
P(Y=1 \mid T=B) > P(Y=1 \mid T=A).
$$

But **is this sufficient evidence** to conclude that treatment B **causes** better outcomes?

---

### The "Simple Statistical Model" Fallacy

* Predictive associations (\$P(Y \mid T)\$) do not imply causal effects (\$P(Y \mid do(T))\$).
* Another hospital might report:

  * \$P(Y=1 \mid T=B) \approx 1\$, \$P(Y=1 \mid T=A) \approx 0\$
  * Yet still \$P(Y=1 \mid do(T=B)) \approx 0\$.
* A simple predictive model can mislead if hidden confounders drive both \$T\$ and \$Y\$.
* **Model simplicity ≠ causal validity**:

  * Occam’s razor (favoring simpler explanations) is unreliable in causal inference.
  * What looks like the simplest explanation can be wrong when confounding variables exists.

### Structural Causal Modeling

- Our desire for incorrectly interpreting the data comes from us trying to describe the **data generation** process.
    - Often, without any evidence to prefer one generation process over another one
- The data often says little about the data generation process
- The data generation process generally comes from prior domain knowledge

**Structural Causal Modeling**

Structural Causal Modeling (SCM) is a formal way to describe what we know about the data generation process.

- Structural Causal Modeling is a combination of data generation equations and their graphical representation
    - Think of SCM as a description of the *code* that generated the data

### Causal Effects

What happens with $T$ if we force $do(T=B)$, i.e., we "force" treatment B on patients (regardless of their kidney stone condition). 
- This  "forcing" is called:
    - An **intervention** if it is done before our data is collected (e.g., to a new person).
        - Example: Clinical trials. Volunteers in the trial are forced to either take the drug or take the placebo.
    - **Counterfactual reasoning** if it is done after the data is collected. That is, we consider an alternative reality that goes against some fact in our data.

[(Galles & Pearl (1998))](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1009602825894) shows that any data generation process can be described through a causal DAG.

## Causal Adjustment: Predicting Causal Effects

- **Goal:** We want to predict $P(Y|\text{do}(T=A))$
    - That is, we want to predict what happens to the probability distribution of $Y$ if we force $T=A$.
    
### **Causal Adjustment Formula (Adjustment for Direct Causes, Theorem 3.2.2 of (Pearl 2009))**:
- Suppose $Y$ is any set of random variables disjoint with $(T \cup C)$, where $C = \text{PA}(T)$ includes all direct parents of $T$ on the causal DAG.
- Then, $$P(Y = y|\text{do}(T=A)) = \sum_{c} P(Y = y|C=c,T=A)P(C=c), \qquad \forall y \in \mathbb{Y}$$
    
    

- Note the difference bettween the adjustment formula above and a *standard conditional probability* statement: $$P(Y = y|T=A) = \sum_{c} P(Y = y|C=c,T=A)P(C=c|T=A).$$
    
<center><img src="Kidney.png" width=500/></center>


**Causal Adjustment Formula Example:**
- T = Treatment: A or B
- Y = Treatment outcome: 0-does not recover, 1-recovers
- S = Kidney stone size: small, large
Let's assume the following causal DAG:
<center><img src="DAG-CAL.png" width=150px></center>
Let's compare $P(Y = 1|\text{do}(T=t))$ against $P(Y = 1| T=t)$, $t \in \{A,B\}$.
<center><img src="Kidney.png" width=500/></center>



- $P(Y=1|T=A)= 0.78$
- $P(Y=1|\text{do}(T=A))= \sum_{s \in \{\text{small,large}\}} P(Y = 1|S=s,T=A)P(S=s) = 0.93 \times 0.51 + 0.73 \times 0.49 = 0.832$
- $P(Y=1|T=B)= 0.83$
- $P(Y=1|\text{do}(T=B))= \sum_{s \in \{\text{small,large}\}} P(Y = 1|S=s,T=B)P(S=s) = 0.87 \times 0.51 + 0.69 \times 0.49 = 0.7818$

## Zillow Offers: A Case Study in Failed Predictive Modeling for Interventions

- Zillow launched the _Zillow's Offers_ program, aimed to revolutionize the real estate market by using machine learning to predict home values and streamline the home buying and selling process.
   - While the concept seemed promising, its implementation faced significant challenges, ultimately leading to the program's discontinuation in late 2021.
   - This case study explores what went wrong, highlighting the importance of understanding the limitations of predictive models to provide **suggestions for interventions**.

Simple Causal Modeling:
- $Z \in \{0,1\}$: House is in the market (0 = false, 1 = true)
- $I \in \{0,1\}$: House has serious issues = 1, no serious issues = 0
- $B \in \{0,1\}$: How buyers (in average) will like (1) or dislike (0) the house
- X = House characteristics, neighborhood, recent selling prices
- $Y \in \mathbb{R}$ = House price

### **What Zillow observes:** $P(Y|X,Z=1)$

### **Goal:** Estimate $P(Y|X,\text{do}(Z=1))$.

<center><img src="DoZillow.png" width=800px></center>

- It's important to acknowledge that "house has serious issues" ($I$), something buyers will not like, acts as a confounder in the relationship between house characteristics and price.

**Problem statement.**
Zillow observes prices only for homes that list, so the *observed* distribution is

$$P(Y\mid X,Z=1).$$

Their goal is to estimate the **interventional quantity**:

$$P(Y\mid X,\text{do}(Z=1)),$$

i.e., the price the same house would fetch *if* Zillow were to make (and thereby induce) an unsolicited offer regardless of its natural listing decision. The gap

$$P(Y\mid X,Z{=}1)\;\neq\;P(Y\mid X,\text{do}(Z{=}1))$$

arises when parents of $Z$—in particular $I$ (issues)—also affect $Y$ because the dependency $Z\leftarrow I\to Y$ not eliminated by conditioning on $X$ alone.

**Causal adjustment.**
If all parents of $Z$ in the causal DAG are observed,
$$P(Y\mid X,\text{do}(Z=1))=\sum_{i \in \{0,1\}} P(Y \mid Z=1,X,I=i)\,P(I=i)$$
is the correct formula to estimate the price of a house Zillow makes an offer.


## Let's see this in practice

- Zillow has gathered data to train a model for predicting house prices.
- The company plans to use this model as part of its "Zillow-Backed Offer" strategy.
    - For each house, Zillow will predict its price using its pricing model, and offer the owner 80% of the predicted price
    - The goal is to sell it to buyers at the full predicted price and make a 20% gross profit.

The dataset has $n$ houses:
- House $i$ has the following features ${\bf x}_i$:
    - **`house_size`**: The total area of the house in square feet.
    - **`num_bedrooms`**: The number of bedrooms in the house.
    - **`num_bathrooms`**: The number of bathrooms in the house.
    - **`age`**: The age of the house, measured in years.
    - **`neighborhood_rating`**: A quality rating for the house's neighborhood, scored on a scale of 1 to 10, based on factors such as local amenities, safety, and school quality.
- In our dataset, a house with $I=1$ sells for half of the price of a house with $I=0$.
- The value of $I$ is hidden from Zillow.
- Zillow only only sees $({\bf x}_i,y_i)$ if house $i$ is in training and ${\bf x}_i$ if house $i$ is in validation.

This model aims to streamline pricing and improve Zillow's buying and selling processes.
    

- **Also note that houses on the market generally have no serious issues $I=0$.**

# Calculate the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) to measure the average magnitude of prediction errors
# - RMSE is computed as the square root of the mean squared differences between the actual and predicted values.
rmse = np.sqrt(((y_val - y_val_pred)**2).mean())

# Calculate the R-squared (R²) score to measure how well the model explains the variance in the target variable
# - `r2_score` is a standard metric where a value of 1 indicates perfect predictions, and 0 indicates no explanatory power.
r2 = r2_score(y_val, y_val_pred)

# - MAPE is the mean of absolute percentage errors, multiplied by 100 for a percentage value.
mape = np.mean(np.abs((y_val - y_val_pred) / y_val)) * 100

### Zillow Case Study Conclusion

- The root cause of this failure was likely a misunderstanding of causality by Zillow's data scientists.
    - Homeowners who accepted Zillow's offers were often those aware of subtle, hidden issues with their properties ($I=1$)
    - These issues are not captured by the features used to train the predictive model $X$.
    - These homeowners typically avoided putting their houses on the market because they would not sell for the prices they would see in their neighborhood for similar houses.
    - This mismatch between the model's predictions and real-world outcomes caused significant financial loss to Zillow.
